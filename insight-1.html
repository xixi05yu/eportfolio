Key Insight 01

Research Is About Curiosity and Learning, Not Having a Perfect Answer

00Overview

Before I had any real experience with research, I had a very unrealistic idea of what it was supposed to look like. A lot of that came from zombie movies. In those movies, a disease suddenly spreads across the world, everything becomes chaotic, and eventually a small group of scientists finds the one antidote that saves everyone. That was the version of research I had in my head. I thought research had to be something huge, dramatic, and world-changing. I assumed that to be a “real researcher,” I had to come up with a completely new discovery or solve a problem that nobody else could solve.

Because of that, I used to think smaller projects did not count as research. If I was only improving something that already existed, testing a limited idea, or finding out that an approach did not work, I did not see much value in it. I thought research was about already being smart enough to know the answer. Through my experiences in CSCE 585 and at the Supercomputing 2025 Conference, I learned that research is almost the opposite. Research begins when someone admits that they do not know something and is curious enough to investigate it.

01Concept: Epistemic Curiosity

Epistemic curiosity

The desire to understand something you do not yet know: being interested in questions, paying attention to evidence, and being willing to change your original opinion.

The concept that best connects these experiences is epistemic curiosity. In normal words, epistemic curiosity is the desire to understand something you do not yet know. It means being interested in questions, asking for help, paying attention to evidence, and being willing to change your original opinion. It also means being comfortable saying, “I do not understand this yet.” That was difficult for me at first because I thought not understanding something meant that I did not belong in research. I now understand that not knowing is actually where research starts.

02Within the Classroom: CSCE 585

My within-the-classroom experience was in CSCE 585, where my group completed a project called AlphaEvolve Clone. Our project was inspired by AlphaEvolve, a system that uses artificial intelligence to improve computer code. We wanted to see whether a smaller version of this type of system could work using more limited and realistic computer resources. Instead of needing an enormous supercomputer, we tested whether smaller AI models could repeatedly suggest changes to code, evaluate the results, and gradually create a better version.

One way to think about our project is like revising a paper. You start with a first draft, make a small change, check whether it improved the paper, and keep the better version. Then you repeat that process. Our system did something similar with computer programs. It used an AI coding model to suggest code changes, tested whether the changes worked, and kept the better versions as a starting point for later attempts. The system could make gradual improvements by learning from what had worked in earlier versions.

My role was focused on understanding, explaining, and presenting the results. This made me pay attention not only to whether the system “won” or “lost,” but also to what the results actually meant. We found that our system could make useful improvements when the starting code already used the correct general approach. For example, if the code had the right main idea but could be made faster or more efficient, our system could improve it over several attempts. However, it struggled when the original code needed a completely different solution. It could make smaller revisions, but it was not always able to make a major jump to a new algorithm or completely new way of solving the problem.

WTC Artifact: AlphaEvolve Clone: CSCE 585 Final Presentation. Our group’s results slides show both where the system improved code and where it reached its limits.

At first, I saw that limitation as a negative result. I thought that if our system could not solve every problem or discover the best possible solution, then maybe the project was not successful. However, this was one of the most important things I learned from the class. A limitation is still a result. Our project helped show what this kind of AI-based system can do well and where it still struggles. It gave us a more realistic understanding of its strengths and weaknesses. Instead of saying, “It failed,” I learned to ask better questions: Why did it work in some situations but not others? What kind of changes can it make? What would need to be different for it to make bigger improvements?

A limitation is still a result.

That experience changed how I viewed research. I started to understand that research is not about proving that your original idea was correct. It is about testing an idea honestly and learning from the answer, even when the answer is not what you expected. The AlphaEvolve project showed me that a research project does not have to create a cure for a zombie disease or solve the biggest problem in the world to matter. It can be a focused question about a specific system. Even a result showing that something has limits can help future researchers know what to improve next.

03Beyond the Classroom: Supercomputing 2025

My beyond-the-classroom experience was presenting my research at the Supercomputing 2025 Conference. I presented AutoSlim, a project focused on making specialized computer hardware more efficient. The project looks at large graphs, which are basically maps made of points and connections. These graphs can represent information used in areas such as cybersecurity, biology, and language processing. As the graphs become bigger, they can require more memory, power, and space in the hardware that processes them.

AutoSlim uses machine learning to find parts of those graphs that may be unnecessary or rarely used. The goal is to remove those unnecessary parts while keeping the important parts working correctly. In simpler terms, it is like cleaning out a crowded storage room: you do not want to throw away something important, but you also do not want to keep hundreds of things that are not being used. By simplifying the graphs, the project can reduce the amount of hardware needed and make the system more efficient. The project showed reductions in hardware-resource use of up to 40 percent.

BTC Artifact: AutoSlim: SC25 Research Poster. This is the poster I presented at Supercomputing 2025. Its results section shows the graph pruning and hardware savings described above.

Presenting AutoSlim was important because it made research feel more real to me. Before the conference, I still thought of research mostly as something done by people who were already experts. At Supercomputing 2025, I attended presentations from researchers whose work was much deeper and more advanced than what I was currently doing. There were moments when I honestly did not understand what the presenter was talking about. The presentations were very detailed, and other people in the audience were asking thoughtful questions that showed they understood the work at a much deeper level than I did.

Instead of making me feel like I should not be there, that experience helped me realize that I still have a lot to learn. I saw that even experienced researchers do not know everything. They ask questions, challenge ideas, learn from each other, and continue building on earlier work. I was amazed that people could be so interested in a very specific topic and could have long discussions about details that I had never considered. It showed me how much depth there is in research and how many directions one topic can lead to.

The conference also helped me realize that research can mean different things to different people. One person may be studying a very theoretical question, another may be improving a small part of a computer system, and another may be focused on how to make existing technology more useful or efficient. None of those projects has to be a dramatic, world-saving discovery to be meaningful. What makes them research is the curiosity behind them: they begin with a question, use evidence to explore it, and add something to what other people can understand or improve.

04The Connection

CSCE 585 and Supercomputing 2025 connect because both experiences taught me to be more comfortable with uncertainty. In the AlphaEvolve project, I learned that it is okay if a system has limitations, because those limitations can lead to new questions. At the conference, I learned that it is okay if I do not understand everything yet, because research is full of people who are still learning and asking questions. The class showed me the process of investigating a question, while the conference showed me that research is also a larger community of people sharing what they have learned.

05Outcome & Impact

My original zombie-movie view of research was that someone has to already know enough to create the miracle solution. Now I see research differently. It is not about having all the answers. It is about being curious enough to ask a question, honest enough to accept the results, and willing to keep learning when you realize how much more there is to understand.

Outcome: How I Changed

I no longer measure research by whether it produces a dramatic breakthrough. I now treat limitations and unexpected results as findings, and I am comfortable saying “I do not understand this yet” and asking questions.

Impact: Moving Forward

As I prepare for a master’s degree, I will approach new research with focused questions, report results honestly, including their limitations, and engage with the research community by asking questions instead of waiting until I feel like an expert.
