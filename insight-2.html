Key Insight 02

Hands-On Experience Turned Knowledge Into Real Skills

00Overview

I have never been someone who learns best by only reading papers, taking notes, and studying for exams. I can learn information that way, but it does not always stay with me for long. I understand something much better when I can actually do it myself. I learn by building, testing, making mistakes, fixing them, and seeing why something works. Through CSCE 201 and my AutoSlim research with Dr. Rasha Karakchi, I learned that hands-on experience is not separate from learning. It is the way I turn information from a class into skills that I can actually use.

01Within the Classroom: CSCE 201

When I took CSCE 201: Introduction to Computer Security, I was introduced to important cybersecurity ideas, including threats, vulnerabilities, attacks, privacy, and the ways computer systems can be protected. At first, many of these ideas felt like definitions I needed to remember for homework or an exam. I learned about concepts such as confidentiality, integrity, and availability. In simple terms, confidentiality means keeping information private, integrity means keeping information correct and unchanged, and availability means making sure people can access a system when they need it.

The class became more meaningful when I had opportunities to apply those ideas instead of only reading about them. My strongest within-the-classroom artifact is Homework 2, where I set up a virtual machine, verified a downloaded file using SHA-1, and safely tested malware samples using antivirus software and VirusTotal. A virtual machine is basically a separate computer environment running inside your actual computer. It allowed me to work in a controlled space without putting my own computer at risk. SHA-1 verification helped me check whether a downloaded file had been changed or corrupted by comparing its digital fingerprint with the expected one.

WTC Artifact: CSCE 201 Homework 2: Virtual Machines, SHA-1 Verification & Malware Testing. It documents the virtual machine setup, the file-fingerprint check, and the safe malware scans.

This assignment stood out because it made cybersecurity feel real. Instead of only reading that harmful files can damage a computer, I had to think about how someone can work with suspicious material safely. I also saw why checking a file before opening it matters. If the file’s fingerprint does not match the expected value, that could mean it was changed, incomplete, or unsafe. This was a small assignment, but it helped me understand that cybersecurity is not only a set of terms. It is a way of thinking carefully about what could go wrong, how to test it safely, and how to protect a system before there is a problem.

A framework that connects this experience to my research is experiential learning. Experiential learning means that people learn through a cycle: they have an experience, reflect on what happened, develop a better understanding, and then test that understanding again. In CSCE 201, I did not just learn what virtual machines, file verification, and malware were. I used the tools, thought about the results, and connected them back to why computer security matters. The experience made the concepts easier to remember because I had done something with them.

03Finding a Mentor

CSCE 201 was also important because it connected me with Dr. Rasha Karakchi. Before taking her class, I had been working with a robotics professor. When that professor left the university, I knew I wanted to continue doing research but was not sure where I would find another opportunity. At the end of class one day, Dr. Karakchi told the class that she had many research ideas and wanted undergraduate students to work with her. I was excited because I needed another research opportunity, so I emailed her.

That email became one of the most important decisions I made in college.

That email became one of the most important decisions I made in college. I started working with Dr. Karakchi and eventually became involved in AutoSlim. I was handed the project after a graduated student had already worked on it, so I did not begin with a blank page or immediately understand every part of the project. I had to learn what the existing code was doing, understand the data that the system used, and figure out how my work fit into the larger project. This was a more realistic version of hands-on learning. In class, assignments usually have clear instructions and a correct answer. In research, I often had to understand an unfamiliar problem first and then decide what to try next.

04Beyond the Classroom: AutoSlim Research

AutoSlim is a software framework that simplifies large graph-based systems. A graph can be thought of as a map made of points and connections. In this project, the points and connections represent parts of a system that processes patterns in data. Those patterns can be useful in areas such as cybersecurity, where systems may need to search through large amounts of information quickly. As the graphs become larger, they can contain unnecessary connections that make the system more complicated and use more resources than needed.

The purpose of AutoSlim is to identify connections that are less important and remove them while preserving the important behavior of the system. In simple terms, it is like cleaning out a crowded closet. You do not want to throw away anything important, but you also do not want to keep items that are never used and only take up space. AutoSlim uses data and machine learning to help decide what can be removed. The goal is to make the system smaller and more efficient without breaking what it is supposed to do.

My work on AutoSlim was on the software and coding side, not the hardware-testing side. I did not do the Vivado or FPGA hardware testing. My hands-on role involved working with the Python and machine-learning code, creating or preparing graph and data files, and testing the software results. The workflow begins by generating graph data and converting it into structured files that the software can analyze. I worked with code that processes those data files, removes lower-value connections based on a chosen threshold, runs machine-learning analysis, and saves the results as charts, logs, and summaries.

BTC Artifacts: the AutoSlim source code (graph generation, CSV conversion, and the Random Forest pruning script) and the research paper describing the method and results.

Because I received an existing project from a former graduated student, part of my learning was understanding code that someone else had already written. I had to read through the code, trace what happened at each step, and learn how the different files connected to each other. I also had to make changes to the Python and machine-learning parts of the project, prepare data, run tests, and look at whether the results made sense. This was very different from memorizing material for an exam. If the code did not run, the output looked wrong, or the test did not produce the result I expected, I could not simply move on. I had to find the problem, think about possible causes, change something, and test it again.

This is where research taught me skills that are difficult to gain from notes alone. I learned how to be patient with a problem that may not have an immediate answer. I learned how to break a large task into smaller pieces instead of becoming overwhelmed by the entire project. I learned that testing is not just the last step after the work is finished. Testing is part of the learning process because it tells me what I still need to understand or improve. I also became more comfortable with failure. When an experiment or piece of code does not work, that does not automatically mean I am bad at research. It means I have more information about what needs to be investigated.

CSCE 201 and AutoSlim connect because the class gave me a beginning understanding of computer security and real-world computing problems, while research gave me the chance to apply technical skills to a much larger and more open-ended problem. The connection is not that every topic from CSCE 201 directly became part of AutoSlim. Instead, CSCE 201 introduced me to the importance of protecting and improving computer systems, gave me hands-on experience working safely with technology, and, most importantly, connected me with the mentor who gave me the opportunity to continue learning through research.

06Outcome & Impact

AutoSlim showed me that I enjoy the process of solving problems over time. I enjoy learning from failures, making changes, testing again, and slowly working toward a solution. When I began college, I did not expect to become so involved in research. I originally saw research as an opportunity to add experience to my resume. Now, I have contributed to multiple papers, presented research posters at symposiums and conferences, and am working toward becoming a first author on a journal paper. More importantly, I have learned that research is where I feel most engaged because it allows me to learn through doing.

This experience changed how I view my own abilities. I am no longer only a student who learns information for a grade. I am someone who can take what I learn in a classroom, apply it to a real problem, learn from mistakes, and contribute to a larger project. Hands-on research has given me technical skills, but it has also given me confidence that I can keep learning even when the problem is unfamiliar or difficult.

Outcome: How I Changed

I see myself as someone who can apply classroom knowledge to an open-ended problem, learn from failed tests, and contribute to a larger project, not only as a student learning for a grade.

Impact: Moving Forward

In graduate school I will keep learning by building, testing, and iterating as I work toward first-author research. As a future TA, I plan to give students active practice, not just notes. That idea is at the center of my Leadership proposal.
