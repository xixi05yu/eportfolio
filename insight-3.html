Key Insight 03

Communication Means Meeting People Where They Are

WTC · EDLP 520 The Teacher as Manager Concept · Strengths-Based, Adaptive Communication BTC · UNIV 101 Peer Leader

00Overview

During my freshman year, communication was difficult for me. I was a shy person, and the idea of talking to other people in class used to make me very nervous. I would usually wait for someone else to talk to me first because I was scared of saying the wrong thing or making the conversation awkward. This made it hard for me to make friends. I was not someone who would walk into a room, introduce myself, and immediately start talking to people. I would stay quiet, even when I wanted to be included.

At the time, I thought being good at communication meant being outgoing, loud, and able to talk to anyone easily. Since that was not naturally me, I did not think I was good at it. Over time, though, I learned that communication is not only about being the person who talks the most. It is also about listening, understanding where another person is coming from, explaining things in a way they can understand, and recognizing that not everyone is ready to speak at the same time or in the same way.

As I became more involved at the University of South Carolina, I slowly put myself in situations that required me to communicate more. I became involved in peer mentorship through MCEC, served as VP of Finance for ALD, became a UNIV 101 Peer Leader, and even applied to be an RA, even though I did not get the position. Each experience made me interact with more people and pushed me outside my comfort zone. I did not suddenly become an extremely outgoing person, but I became more willing to start conversations, speak up, and take responsibility when other people needed help.

01Within the Classroom: EDLP 520

My within-the-classroom experience for this insight was EDLP 520: The Teacher as Manager. This course was connected directly to my role as a UNIV 101 Peer Leader. It was not just a class where I learned leadership theories and then forgot about them after an assignment. It was a space where I could reflect on what was happening in my UNIV 101 class, think about challenges I was having, and learn how to become a better support person for first-year students. My UNIV 101 class was like the place where I practiced being a Peer Leader, while EDLP 520 helped me understand why certain communication and leadership strategies mattered.

The course taught skills such as facilitation, helping students, making referrals, conflict resolution, lesson planning, leadership styles, and understanding first-year students. These topics helped me realize that being a Peer Leader is more than standing in front of a classroom and giving students information. Students may need help with academic advising, time management, study skills, procrastination, personal concerns, or simply figuring out where they belong at the university. Giving everyone the exact same explanation is not always enough. I had to learn how to communicate information in different ways depending on what a student needed.

02Concept: Knowing Myself to Support Others

Strengths-based, adaptive communication

EDLP 520’s facilitation and referral skills, combined with the self-assessment in my Leadership Profile (CliftonStrengths: Adaptability, Positivity, Developer, Relator, Empathy), showed me how to adjust my support to each student’s needs instead of giving everyone the same response.

My Leadership Profile from EDLP 520 helped me reflect more honestly on the type of leader and communicator I am. In that assignment, I described myself as “slow warming,” meaning that I may take time to become comfortable around new people. I also identified empathy, positivity, hard work, adaptability, and sharing knowledge as strengths. Looking at those strengths helped me realize that my quieter personality does not prevent me from being a leader. In fact, being someone who listens carefully and notices when another person is uncomfortable can be useful when working with students.

WTC Artifact: EDLP 520 Leadership Profile. It covers my strengths and weaknesses, values, CliftonStrengths, Myers-Briggs type, and my advice to “share everything you know.”

The Leadership Profile also made me think about my own weaknesses. I can take on too many responsibilities, become overwhelmed, and procrastinate when there is too much happening at once. Learning about myself mattered because I cannot communicate effectively with other people if I do not understand how I react to stress, responsibility, or unfamiliar situations. The assignment showed me that leadership is not about being perfect or acting like I have everything figured out. It is about recognizing my strengths and weaknesses, continuing to improve, and using what I know to help others.

03Beyond the Classroom: UNIV 101 Peer Leader

My beyond-the-classroom experience was my actual work as a UNIV 101 Peer Leader. This was where I began to understand how different students are from one another. I got to know students through class check-ins, their written reflections, one-on-one conversations, their lifeline presentations, and activities where they shared their favorite things. These activities helped me learn about their personalities, backgrounds, concerns, and goals. They also showed me that students do not all learn, ask for help, or open up in the same way.

One experience that stood out to me involved students who were quiet during class but opened up more when I spoke with them one-on-one. In a class discussion, a quiet student can seem uninterested or disengaged. However, I learned that quiet does not always mean that someone does not care. Some students need more time before sharing in front of a group. Others may feel more comfortable asking questions privately because they are nervous about being judged or do not want to look like they do not understand something. When I met with students individually, I could learn more about what they were actually experiencing and help them in a way that matched their needs.

Quiet does not always mean that someone does not care.

This changed how I thought about communication. I learned that I cannot assume every student will ask for help in the same way I would. Some students need direct questions, such as, “How are classes going?” or “Is there anything confusing you right now?” Others need to see that it is safe to be honest before they are willing to share. Some may need a conversation, while others may need a clear resource or a step-by-step explanation of what to do next. My role was not to force students to open up. It was to create an environment where they knew they could ask for help when they were ready.

My beyond-the-classroom artifacts are checklists I made for my students on different topics. These artifacts represent an important part of how I communicate with students: making helpful information easier to find and understand. When students are new to college, they may not know what resources exist, who they should contact, or what questions they are even supposed to ask. A resource guide is not just a list of information. It can make a student feel less overwhelmed by showing them that there are specific places, people, and steps available to help them. It also connects to one piece of advice from my Leadership Profile: students come from different backgrounds and do not all begin college with the same knowledge, so it is important to share information instead of assuming they already know it.

BTC Artifacts: Academic Advising Checklist and Off Campus Living Checklist. I made these one-page guides for my UNIV 101 students.

04Connection to Research

This insight also connects to my research experiences. I have been part of several research groups, and each one communicated differently. My first research group was technically a group, but most people worked individually. Everyone had separate tasks, and there was not much check-in from the professor or graduate students. I had to be independent, but there were fewer chances to talk through problems or learn from the work other people were doing. My second research experience, the AutoSlim project, was more individual. I worked directly with my mentor, and we had weekly meetings to discuss my progress. That structure was helpful because I could explain what I had completed, ask questions when I was confused, and receive guidance before a problem became too big. My current research group has a different structure. A graduate student oversees the larger group, while smaller groups of two or three students work more closely together with their own leader-like person. We have weekly check-ins with the graduate student, and when there are larger updates or more difficult problems, we meet with the mentor.

The graduate student in my current group reminds me of the role I try to have with my UNIV 101 students. He shares his experience, explains things that are unfamiliar to us, and helps us work through problems without expecting us to already know everything. He does not just give us an answer and leave. He helps us understand the process, what we should focus on, and what to do when we get stuck. In a similar way, I share my own college experiences with first-year students and help them understand things that may be unfamiliar to them, such as registration, study strategies, time management, campus resources, or how to ask for help.

This comparison helped me understand that communication is important in both leadership and research. In both situations, people may be new, unsure, or afraid to ask questions. A first-year student may not know how to navigate college, just as an undergraduate research student may not understand a new technical topic or know how to begin a task. In both situations, the person with more experience can make a major difference by being approachable, explaining things clearly, checking in regularly, and making it safe to admit confusion.

05Outcome & Impact

This is why communication is not just about speaking well. It is about paying attention to the person in front of you and understanding that different people need different kinds of support. I started college as someone who was afraid to speak first. Through EDLP 520, peer leadership, and my research experiences, I have learned that I can communicate in a way that fits who I am: by listening, sharing what I know, asking questions, and helping people feel more comfortable asking for help themselves.

Outcome: How I Changed

Communication no longer means being the loudest person in the room. I now listen first, offer more than one way for people to engage, and share information instead of assuming others already know it.

Impact: Moving Forward

In research groups and as a future TA, I want to be the approachable, more experienced person who checks in regularly and makes it safe to admit confusion. That goal shapes my Leadership proposal.
